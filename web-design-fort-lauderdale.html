A recent build, and the job it had to do.

Elite Care Recovery is a family business run by two brothers with twenty-three years in orthopedic recovery between them. They rent cold and compression therapy equipment to patients coming out of surgery and deliver it to the door.

The site was not there to find customers

Surgeons were already sending patients. The website's job was to survive being checked afterwards, by a patient who has just been told a name and wants to know whether these people are real.

Read from a hospital bed, on hospital wifi

That is who the design was drawn for. One thumb, a small screen, a bad connection, and often the night before an operation. Every page loads in under a second, which is why it is static files with no content system sitting behind them.

A reservation form that works at 3am

Surgery dates do not respect office hours. Patients book their equipment when they think of it, which is frequently the middle of the night, so the form had to be the easiest thing on the page rather than the last thing on it.

Nothing to maintain and no hosting bill

Four core pages, no plugins to update and no monthly server to pay for. A small business should not inherit a maintenance problem as the price of having a website, and this one did not.

Things Fort Lauderdale owners want settled first.

Almost all my work is referrals. What is a website going to do for me?

It closes the referrals you are already getting. When somebody passes your name along, the next thing that happens is a search, and whatever comes up is the first impression you never got to make in person. A dated site, or none at all, quietly costs you a share of the introductions your customers are working hard to give you. That is a different job from advertising, and it is a much easier one.

Will you meet me in person in Fort Lauderdale?

Yes, when it earns the trip. Some jobs genuinely need it: photographing a yard, walking a dining room, sitting with a team that will be updating the site themselves. Most kickoffs and reviews go faster on a screen share where we can both see the thing we are talking about. I am reachable by phone, text and email in Eastern time either way.

My site exists but it is old. Repair or replace?

Depends what is wrong with it. Slow, ugly and out of date is often repairable and much cheaper than starting over. Built on something abandoned, or dependent on a plugin that no longer has an author, usually is not, because you would be paying me to maintain a countdown. I look before I quote, and I have talked people out of a rebuild more than once.

What moves the price up or down?

Three things, in this order. How many pages, because each one is a piece of writing and a layout. Whether you have usable photographs, since stock imagery of somebody else's boat is visible from a mile away. And whether the site has to do something beyond informing, like taking a booking or a deposit. Everything is listed on the pricing page, including what costs extra, so there is nothing to discover on the invoice.

Do you understand the marine trade?

I understand the shape of the business: long sales cycles, high ticket, buyers who are frequently not in Florida, and a reputation that lives on the dock rather than online. I will not claim to know a refit schedule better than you do. What I do is get that knowledge out of your head and onto a page a prospective owner can read on a phone from another time zone.

Do I need my site in Spanish up here?

Only if your customers are there, and in Fort Lauderdale that varies by trade far more than it does south of the county line. I build bilingual as a matter of course and I write the Spanish myself, so it is available to you at no extra charge. But I am not going to tell you it is mandatory when it may not be. If Spanish-speaking customers are a real part of your market, the argument for doing it properly is set out on the Miami web design page.
